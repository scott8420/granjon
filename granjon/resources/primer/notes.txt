[overshoot]
title: Overshoot -- why round letters are bigger
figure: overshoot.svg
link: Measure o against the x-height | tool:measure:o
link: Checks: Overshoot | report:overshoot
link: The font's overshoot | font:metrics
A circle and a square of the same height do not look the same height. The square
meets the lines along its whole edge; the circle touches them at one point and
curves away, so it looks *smaller*.

So round letters reach a little past the baseline and the x-height (or cap
height): o, O, c, e, s, the bowls of b d p q. That is the overshoot -- about 1 to
1.5 % of the em in a text face, 10 to 15 units at 1000. Pointed shapes overshoot
too, often more: the apex of A, the foot of a pointed v or w.

Too little and o looks small beside n; too much and the rounds bounce at large
sizes. Keep it the same everywhere: the eye compares every round with every other.

In Granjon: the font's overshoot is in Vertical Metrics, the editor shades the
zones, and the Checks report finds rounds that miss them by a unit or two. The
presets' o and O already overshoot.

[stems]
title: Stem weight and contrast -- horizontals are thinner
figure: stems.svg
link: Measure n's stem (click inside it) | tool:measure:n
link: Measure o's sides and top | tool:measure:o
link: This font's stems | font:metrics
Draw a vertical and a horizontal bar of exactly the same thickness and the
horizontal looks *heavier*. So even a sans that looks even all over draws its
horizontal strokes thinner than its vertical stems: the Sans preset uses 88 for
n's stem and 78 across the top of its arch.

The ratio between thick and thin is the *contrast*. A book serif thins its
horizontals to a third or so (the Serif preset: 82 and 30); a display face may go
further. The contrast is a decision about the whole design -- pick it once and
hold it.

Rounds need a touch more weight than straight stems: a curve is at its thickest
only for an instant, so the sides of o are drawn a little heavier than n's stem
(6 % in the presets). Capitals are a little heavier than lowercase, or they look
light beside it.

Measure, don't guess: hold M (or the Measure tool) and click inside a stem; the
readout gives its width. Write the numbers down and keep to them.

[spacing]
title: Round and straight -- spacing n and o
figure: spacing.svg
link: Spacing: nnonoo nnoonn | spacing:nnnn nnonoo nnoonn
link: Spacing: HHOHOO HHOOHH | spacing:HHHH HHOHOO HHOOHH
link: Apply spacing keys to every glyph | action:font-metrics-keys
Spacing is half of a typeface. Start with the control letters: n for straight
sides, o for round ones (H and O for capitals).

Two straight stems facing each other need the most room. A round side needs
less, because the white curving away above and below it counts as space too. The
aim is the same *area* of white between every pair, not the same distance --
that is why o's sidebearings are smaller than n's.

A working method (Walter Tracy's): set n's sides so "nnnn" has an even rhythm,
about half the width of its counter between stems. Then set o so that "nnonoo"
and "nnoonn" look even. Then H and O, the same way.

Everything else follows from these four: a straight side like n, a round side
like o. The presets wrote those rules as spacing keys -- b takes n's left and o's
right, d the other way round, u is n turned over -- so when n and o change, one
click passes it on.

[joins]
title: Joins and ink traps
figure: joins.svg
link: Look at n's joins | glyph:n
link: Look at p's joins | glyph:p
link: Look at v | glyph:v
Where two strokes meet, the ink piles up: n's arch leaving its stem, the bowl of
p meeting its stem, the bottom of v. Drawn at full weight, a join looks like a
dark knot.

So a stroke *thins* as it enters a join. n's arch leaves the stem thinner than it
is across the top, and the inside curve cuts in deeper than the outside one --
look at the presets' n and p, where the counter bites into the stem.

At small sizes and in heavy weights, go further: cut a notch into the inside
corner -- an *ink trap* -- so it does not fill in when printed small or rendered
on screen. The heavier the weight, the deeper the cut. At display sizes the
trap shows; some designers keep it as part of the look.

[optical]
title: Optical centre -- a little above the middle
figure: optical.svg
link: H and its crossbar guide | glyph:H
link: The font's guides and metrics | font:metrics
The eye puts the middle of a shape a little *above* its true middle. A crossbar
at exactly half the cap height looks low. So the bars of H, E, F, A and the
middle of B, P, R sit slightly higher -- the presets put H's bar at 52 % of the
cap height, and left a "crossbar" guide there so every bar can line up with it.

The same illusion makes letters with two stacked parts look top-heavy when the
parts are equal: draw the top of B, S, 8, 3, X a little smaller (narrower, and
shorter) than the bottom, and the letter stands.

A shape centred by numbers is not centred to the eye. Trust the eye, then make
it consistent with a guide.

[order]
title: Drawing order -- where to start
figure: order.svg
link: Open the six control glyphs | glyph:n,o,H,O,p,v
link: Spacing: nnonoo HHOHOO | spacing:nnonoo HHOHOO
link: The Overview | view:overview
Don't start at A. Draw the *control characters* first, and space them with each
other before anything else:

n and o set the lowercase: stem, arch, round, weight, contrast, spacing. Then H
and O for the capitals, p for the descender, v for the diagonals.

Then grow families from them. n gives h m u r; o gives c e; n and o together give
b d p q; v gives w y x; H gives E F I L T; O gives C G Q D. The rest are cousins
of those.

Test in words early -- the Spacing strip, the Preview -- not glyph by glyph. A
letter is only right next to other letters.

Accented letters come free: the presets added them as components (base + mark),
so they fill in as you draw the base letters and the marks.
